**Global issue**: Assertion of female identity and agency in patriarchal systems of control

**Thesis**: In Duffy’s poem, Pygmalion’s Bride and Ibsen’s A Doll’s House, the protagonists’ final acts of departure function as deliberate, performative rebellion.

# A World’s Wife
1. I showed no scratch, no scrape, no scar.
2. He propped me up on pillows,
3. jawed all night.
4. My heart was ice, was glass.
5. His voice was gravel, hoarse.
6. He talked white black.
7. So I changed tack,
8. grew warm, like candle wax,
9. kissed back,
10. was soft, was pliable,
11. began to moan,
12. got hot, got wild,
13. arched, coiled, writhed,
14. begged for his child,
15. and at the climax
16. screamed my head off—
17. all an act.
18. And haven’t seen him since.
19. Simple as that.

# A Doll’s House
**Helmer**. This is the end, then! Nora, will you never think of me
any more?
**Nora**. Yes, of course. I shall often think of you and the children
and this house.
**Helmer**. May I write to you, Nora?
**Nora**. No. Never. You mustn't do that.
**Helmer**. But at least you must let me send you—
**Nora**. Nothing. Nothing.
**Helmer**. But if you should need help—?
**Nora**. I tell you, no. I don't accept things from strangers.
**Helmer**. Nora-can I never be anything but a stranger to you?
**Nora** (picks up her bag). Oh, Torvald! Then the miracle of
miracles would have to happen.
**Helmer**. The miracle of miracles!
**Nora**. You and I would both have to change so much that-oh,
**Torvald**, I don't believe in miracles any longer.
**Helmer**. But I want to believe in them. Tel.me. We should have
to change so much that-1
**Nora**. That life together between us two
could become a marri-
age. Goodbye.
*She goes out through the hall.*
**Helmer** (sinks down on a chair by the door and buries his
face in his hands). Nora! Nora! (looks round and gets up) Empty! She's
gone! (a hope strikes him) The miracle of miracles—?
*The street door is slammed shut downstairs.*

# 10 Point Outline
- INT: GI: Female agency in patriarchy. Duffy (performative escape) vs. Ibsen (ideological rupture). Thesis: Departure as deliberate rebellion.
- LIT C1: Duffy’s triple negatives + hard consonance = woman as manufactured object. Insight: Patriarchy demands female erasure for control.
- LIT W1: Motif of theatricality across The World’s Wife (e.g., Mrs. Midas). Insight: Performance becomes a survival tactic & covert power.
- LIT C2: “all an act” + abrupt closure. Insight/Eval: Agency as performative deception; effective shock but risks reading as cold.
- LIT W2: Myth subversion in collection. Insight: Rewriting patriarchal narratives is itself an act of agency.
- NLIT C1: Ibsen’s repetitive negation (“No. Never”). Insight: Language dismantles relational patriarchy.
- NLIT W1: Nora’s earlier doll-performance. Insight: Economic secrecy prefigures ideological break.
- NLIT C2: Slamming door + unresolved dash. Insight/Eval: Sonic/visual rupture; powerful symbol but leaves systemic change uncertain.
- NLIT W2: Ibsen’s female exodus trope (Hedda, etc.). Insight: Exit must be public to challenge social hypocrisy.
- CON: Contrast covert vs. confrontational rebellion. Both claim self-authorship. Lasting reflection on rebellion’s forms.

# Script
**(0:00–1:00) INTRODUCTION**
"Good morning. The global issue I am exploring is the assertion of female identity and agency within patriarchal systems of control. I will examine Carol Ann Duffy’s poem Pygmalion’s Bride from The World’s Wife and Henrik Ibsen’s play A Doll’s House. While both works conclude with a female protagonist’s decisive departure, they represent divergent modes of rebellion: Duffy’s speaker employs performative deception to escape, whereas Ibsen’s Nora enacts an ideological rupture. I will argue that their final acts are deliberate, performative rebellions that reclaim narrative and bodily autonomy from patriarchal control."

**(1:00–2:15) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**
"First, in Pygmalion’s Bride, Duffy immediately establishes the speaker’s objectification. The opening line—'I showed no scratch, no scrape, no scar'—uses triple negation and hard consonance to construct her as a smooth, unmarked artifact. This reflects the patriarchal desire to erase female history and identity, presenting woman as a blank canvas for male fantasy. However, Duffy’s first-person narration is itself subversive; it grants interiority to a figure mythically silenced. The insight here is that patriarchal control operates through manufactured perfection, demanding the removal of all traces of independent existence."

**(2:15–3:30) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**
"Zooming out to Duffy’s collection The World’s Wife, we see that theatrical performance is a recurring tactic for female agency. In poems like Mrs. Midas or Mrs. Lazarus, women endure then exit legendary male narratives through cunning or decisive action. In Pygmalion’s Bride, the speaker’s transformation—from 'glass' to 'candle wax' to wildly performative passion—mirrors this. Duffy’s wider motif suggests that under patriarchy, agency is often exercised covertly through role-play, a strategic performance that preserves the self while deceiving the oppressor."

**(3:30–4:30) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**
"The climax of the poem is a fabricated ecstasy: 'and at the climax / screamed my head off— / all an act.' The abrupt enjambment and colloquial finale—'Simple as that.'—create a tone of chilling detachment. This reveals a profound layer of the global issue: agency can be a performative deception. She weaponizes Pygmalion’s own desire to secure her freedom. Evaluating this, Duffy’s choice is highly effective in its shock value, subverting the myth of male creation. However, one could argue it portrays agency as purely reactive and isolated, offering no model for collective resistance."

**(4:30–5:00) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**
"This subversion is central to Duffy’s project. By rewriting myths from the female perspective, she reclaims narrative authority. The speaker’s final exit—'And haven’t seen him since.'—resonates with poems like Mrs. Faust, where women walk away from legendary men. The wider insight is that in a patriarchal literary tradition, rewriting the story is itself an act of liberation. Departure is not just physical but narrative."

**(5:00–6:00) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**
"Shifting to Ibsen’s A Doll’s House, Nora’s rebellion is confrontational, not covert. In the final scene, her dialogue is a series of decisive negations: 'No. Never. You mustn’t… Nothing. Nothing.' The staccato syntax and firm declaratives signal a seismic shift in linguistic power. When she states, 'I don’t accept things from strangers,' she linguistically exiles Torvald from the marital sphere. This reveals that patriarchal control is embedded in relational language, and agency requires its dismantling."

**(6:00–6:45) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**
"Throughout the play, Nora’s performance as a 'doll' or 'songbird' is a survival tactic within the bourgeois patriarchy. Her secret loan—an act of economic initiative—is the first crack in this performance. This wider pattern shows that her final departure is not impulsive, but the culmination of gradual moral and economic self-reclamation. Ibsen suggests that true agency under patriarchy requires an internal awakening before the physical exit."

**(6:45–7:45) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**
"The rebellion culminates in the iconic stage direction: *She goes out through the hall. f*ollowed by the slamming of the street door. This auditory symbol marks an irreversible, public rupture. It’s paired with Torvald’s desperate, unfinished line: 'The miracle of miracles—?' The unresolved dash leaves the systemic future uncertain. Ibsen’s choice is powerfully effective, creating a lasting cultural symbol of emancipation. However, its brilliance is also its limitation: the slam is an endpoint, not a blueprint for what female self-authorship looks like beyond the door."

**(7:45–8:15) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**
"Zooming out to A Doll’s House as a whole, Nora’s exit is not an isolated event but the culmination of a carefully constructed dramatic arc. Ibsen frames her departure as a public, sonic event—the iconic slamming door is not just a stage direction but the play’s final, resonant punctuation. This choice transforms Nora’s personal rebellion into an audible confrontation with the audience itself. The earlier acts of performance—her doll-like demeanor, her secret loan—create a tension that finds its release only in that decisive sound. The wider insight is that Ibsen uses the theatrical medium itself to argue that patriarchy must be confronted publicly and sonically to provoke societal awakening, contrasting sharply with Duffy’s model of private, silent escape."

**(8:15–9:15) SYNTHESIS & CONCLUSION**
"In conclusion, both texts offer profound insights into female rebellion. Duffy reveals agency as a covert performance of deception, using the oppressor’s script to escape. Ibsen presents agency as an ideological confrontation, rejecting the script entirely. Duffy’s rebellion is interior and narrative; Ibsen’s is public and sonic. Yet both departures are profoundly performative and deliberate, asserting that under patriarchy, the ultimate act of self-possession is often the act of leaving. They collectively expand our understanding of resistance, showing it can be as silent as a rewritten poem or as loud as a door slam echoing through history."
