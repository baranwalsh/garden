**Global issue**: Assertion of female identity and agency in patriarchal systems of control

**Thesis**: In Duffy’s poem, Pygmalion’s Bride and Ibsen’s A Doll’s House, the protagonists’ final acts of departure function as deliberate, performative rebellion.

# A World’s Wife
1. I showed no scratch, no scrape, no scar.
2. He propped me up on pillows,
3. jawed all night.
4. My heart was ice, was glass.
5. His voice was gravel, hoarse.
6. He talked white black.
7. So I changed tack,
8. grew warm, like candle wax,
9. kissed back,
10. was soft, was pliable,
11. began to moan,
12. got hot, got wild,
13. arched, coiled, writhed,
14. begged for his child,
15. and at the climax
16. screamed my head off—
17. all an act.
18. And haven’t seen him since.
19. Simple as that.

# A Doll’s House
**Helmer**. This is the end, then! Nora, will you never think of me
any more?
**Nora**. Yes, of course. I shall often think of you and the children
and this house.
**Helmer**. May I write to you, Nora?
**Nora**. No. Never. You mustn't do that.
**Helmer**. But at least you must let me send you—
**Nora**. Nothing. Nothing.
**Helmer**. But if you should need help—?
**Nora**. I tell you, no. I don't accept things from strangers.
**Helmer**. Nora-can I never be anything but a stranger to you?
**Nora** (picks up her bag). Oh, Torvald! Then the miracle of
miracles would have to happen.
**Helmer**. The miracle of miracles!
**Nora**. You and I would both have to change so much that-oh,
**Torvald**, I don't believe in miracles any longer.
**Helmer**. But I want to believe in them. Tel.me. We should have
to change so much that-1
**Nora**. That life together between us two
could become a marri-
age. Goodbye.
*She goes out through the hall.*
**Helmer** (sinks down on a chair by the door and buries his
face in his hands). Nora! Nora! (looks round and gets up) Empty! She's
gone! (a hope strikes him) The miracle of miracles—?
*The street door is slammed shut downstairs.*

# Script
**(0:00–1:00) INTRODUCTION**
"Good morning. The global issue I am exploring is the assertion of female identity and agency within patriarchal systems of control. I will examine Carol Ann Duffy’s poem Pygmalion’s Bride from The World’s Wife and Henrik Ibsen’s play A Doll’s House. While both works conclude with a female protagonist’s decisive departure, they represent divergent modes of rebellion: Duffy’s speaker employs performative deception to escape, whereas Ibsen’s Nora enacts an ideological rupture. I will argue that their final acts are deliberate, performative rebellions that reclaim narrative and bodily autonomy from patriarchal control."

**(1:00–2:15) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**
"First, in Pygmalion’s Bride, Duffy immediately establishes the speaker’s objectification. The opening line—'I showed no scratch, no scrape, no scar'—uses triple negation and hard consonance to construct her as a smooth, unmarked artifact. This reflects the patriarchal desire to erase female history and identity, presenting woman as a blank canvas for male fantasy. However, Duffy’s first-person narration is itself subversive; it grants interiority to a figure mythically silenced. The insight here is that patriarchal control operates through manufactured perfection, demanding the removal of all traces of independent existence."

**(2:15–3:30) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**
"Zooming out to Duffy’s collection The World’s Wife, we see that theatrical performance is a recurring tactic for female agency. In poems like Mrs. Midas or Mrs. Lazarus, women endure then exit legendary male narratives through cunning or decisive action. In Pygmalion’s Bride, the speaker’s transformation—from 'glass' to 'candle wax' to wildly performative passion—mirrors this. Duffy’s wider motif suggests that under patriarchy, agency is often exercised covertly through role-play, a strategic performance that preserves the self while deceiving the oppressor."

**(3:30–4:30) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**
"The climax of the poem is a fabricated ecstasy: 'and at the climax / screamed my head off— / all an act.' The abrupt enjambment and colloquial finale—'Simple as that.'—create a tone of chilling detachment. This reveals a profound layer of the global issue: agency can be a performative deception. She weaponizes Pygmalion’s own desire to secure her freedom. Evaluating this, Duffy’s choice is highly effective in its shock value, subverting the myth of male creation. However, one could argue it portrays agency as purely reactive and isolated, offering no model for collective resistance."

**(4:30–5:00) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**
"This subversion is central to Duffy’s project. By rewriting myths from the female perspective, she reclaims narrative authority. The speaker’s final exit—'And haven’t seen him since.'—resonates with poems like Mrs. Faust, where women walk away from legendary men. The wider insight is that in a patriarchal literary tradition, rewriting the story is itself an act of liberation. Departure is not just physical but narrative."

**(5:00–6:00) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**
"Shifting to Ibsen’s A Doll’s House, Nora’s rebellion is confrontational, not covert. In the final scene, her dialogue is a series of decisive negations: 'No. Never. You mustn’t… Nothing. Nothing.' The staccato syntax and firm declaratives signal a seismic shift in linguistic power. When she states, 'I don’t accept things from strangers,' she linguistically exiles Torvald from the marital sphere. This reveals that patriarchal control is embedded in relational language, and agency requires its dismantling."

**(6:00–6:45) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**
"Throughout the play, Nora’s performance as a 'doll' or 'songbird' is a survival tactic within the bourgeois patriarchy. Her secret loan—an act of economic initiative—is the first crack in this performance. This wider pattern shows that her final departure is not impulsive, but the culmination of gradual moral and economic self-reclamation. Ibsen suggests that true agency under patriarchy requires an internal awakening before the physical exit."

**(6:45–7:45) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**
"The rebellion culminates in the iconic stage direction: *She goes out through the hall. f*ollowed by the slamming of the street door. This auditory symbol marks an irreversible, public rupture. It’s paired with Torvald’s desperate, unfinished line: 'The miracle of miracles—?' The unresolved dash leaves the systemic future uncertain. Ibsen’s choice is powerfully effective, creating a lasting cultural symbol of emancipation. However, its brilliance is also its limitation: the slam is an endpoint, not a blueprint for what female self-authorship looks like beyond the door."

**(7:45–8:15) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**
"Zooming out to A Doll’s House as a whole, Nora’s exit is not an isolated event but the culmination of a carefully constructed dramatic arc. Ibsen frames her departure as a public, sonic event—the iconic slamming door is not just a stage direction but the play’s final, resonant punctuation. This choice transforms Nora’s personal rebellion into an audible confrontation with the audience itself. The earlier acts of performance—her doll-like demeanor, her secret loan—create a tension that finds its release only in that decisive sound. The wider insight is that Ibsen uses the theatrical medium itself to argue that patriarchy must be confronted publicly and sonically to provoke societal awakening, contrasting sharply with Duffy’s model of private, silent escape."

**(8:15–9:15) SYNTHESIS & CONCLUSION**
"In conclusion, both texts offer profound insights into female rebellion. Duffy reveals agency as a covert performance of deception, using the oppressor’s script to escape. Ibsen presents agency as an ideological confrontation, rejecting the script entirely. Duffy’s rebellion is interior and narrative; Ibsen’s is public and sonic. Yet both departures are profoundly performative and deliberate, asserting that under patriarchy, the ultimate act of self-possession is often the act of leaving. They collectively expand our understanding of resistance, showing it can be as silent as a rewritten poem or as loud as a door slam echoing through history."

# Revised Script (Edited)

**(0:00–1:15) INTRODUCTION**  
"Good morning. Today I will be exploring Carol Ann Duffy’s poem _Pygmalion’s Bride_ and Henrik Ibsen’s play _A Doll’s House_, I will examine how female protagonists navigate and ultimately reject patriarchal confinement. The global issue I will explore is the assertion of female identity and agency within patriarchal systems of control. While both texts conclude with decisive acts of departure, they represent fundamentally different modes of rebellion. Duffy’s speaker enacts a covert, performative escape, using deception to liberate herself. In contrast, Ibsen’s Nora stages a direct, ideological protest, publicly slamming the door on her former life. I will argue that these final acts are deliberate, performative rebellions that reclaim narrative and bodily autonomy, offering insights into how women assert selfhood under oppression."

**(1:15–2:45) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**  
"In _Pygmalion’s Bride_, Duffy immediately establishes the dynamics of patriarchal control through the speaker’s objectified condition. The opening line, 'I showed no scratch, no scrape, no scar' illustrates with triple negation and hard consonance to construct the female figure as a smooth, unmarked artifact. The repetition of the ’s’ and ‘c’ sounds creates a tactile imagery of a polished surface, devoid of imperfection or individuality. This reflects the patriarchal desire to erase female subjectivity, presenting women as a blank canvas for male fantasy. However, Duffy’s strategic use of first-person narration subtly undermines this objectification from within. By granting the silent statue a voice, she creates personhood where none was supposed to exist. The insight here is that patriarchal control often operates through a fantasy of feminine perfection, a perfection that requires the removal of all traces of independent existence, imperfection, or resistance."

**(2:45–4:00) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**  
"Expanding to Duffy’s collection _The World’s Wife_ reveals that strategic performance is a recurring motif for female agency. Across the collection, from _Mrs. Midas_ to _Mrs. Lazarus_, women endure and then exit legendary male narratives through cunning, patient, or decisive action. In _Pygmalion’s Bride_, the speaker’s transformation from ‘glass’ to ‘candle wax’ to performative passion represents the desperation of escape. This wider pattern suggests that within oppressive systems, agency is not always a matter of open defiance. Instead, it can be a covert performance, a survival tactic that preserves the core self while strategically deceiving the oppressor. Theatricality, therefore, becomes a form of power over the oppressor within the restricted agency given."

**(4:00–5:15) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**  
"The poem’s climax demonstrates performative deception: 'and at the climax / screamed my head off— / all an act.' The abrupt enjambment after 'climax' creates a jarring pause, revealing the truth behind the act. The final line, 'Simple as that.', with its colloquial flatness, delivers a tone of cool, almost dismissive detachment as seen with Duffy's witty writing. Referencing the global issue, that agency can be an exercise in performative deception. She weaponises Pygmalion’s own desire to create an opportunity to escape. Evaluating this choice, Duffy’s approach is effective in its subversive shock, completely reversing the power dynamic of the original myth. However, one could critique this portrayal as presenting agency in somewhat isolated and reactive terms. Her rebellion is brilliant but solitary, offering a model of escape rather than transformation of the system itself."

**(5:15–6:00) TEXT 1 – DUFFY, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**  
"This act of subversive rewriting is central to Duffy’s entire poetic project. By reclaiming myths and histories from the female perspective, she engages in an act of narrative reclamation. The speaker’s final, quiet exit—'And haven’t seen him since.'—echoes throughout _The World’s Wife_, where the feminist lens for famous tales allows women to reclaim agency. The wider insight here is that in a cultural landscape shaped by patriarchal narratives, rewriting the story is itself an act of advocacy. Departure is not merely physical; it is literary and historical. To exit the myth is to refuse the role assigned by tradition."

**(6:00–7:15) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 1**  
"Shifting to Ibsen’s _A Doll’s House_, we encounter a rebellion that is confrontational rather than covert. In the final scene, Nora’s language undergoes a dramatic transformation. Her dialogue becomes a series of decisive, repetitive negations: 'No. Never. You mustn’t… Nothing. Nothing.' This staccato syntax and her use of firm rejections is a shift in linguistic authority. When she states, 'I don’t accept things from strangers,' she linguistically exiles Torvald from the intimate sphere of marriage, naming him as an outsider. This reveals the insight that patriarchal control is deeply embedded in relational language and social roles. Agency, therefore, requires the dismantling of that linguistic and relational framework. Nora’s words are the tools of her rebellion."

**(7:15–8:00) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 1**  
"Examining the play as a whole, Nora’s final departure is the culmination of her restrictive experiences. Her earlier performance as the perfect ‘doll-wife’, the songbird, the spendthrift, was a survival tactic within the bourgeois patriarchal home. Her secret act of securing a loan, however, represents the first major crack in this performance, an assertion of economic and moral will that exists outside Torvald’s knowledge or control. This wider pattern demonstrates that her slam of the door is preceded by a quieter, internal slam—a rejection of the ideological foundations of her marriage. Ibsen suggests that true agency begins with an internal awakening to one’s own personhood, which then necessitates external action."

**(8:00–8:45) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, CLOSE ANALYSIS 2 & EVALUATION**  
"The rebellion achieves its iconic form in the final moments of staging. Nora’s exit through the hall is followed by the decisive _slamming of the street door_. This is not merely a sound effect; it is an auditory symbol of irreversible, public rupture. Ibsen pairs this with Torvald’s unfinished, hopeful question—'The miracle of miracles—?'—leaving the sentence dangling in a dash. This unresolved punctuation refuses narrative closure. Evaluating Ibsen’s choices, the slammed door is phenomenally effective, creating one of the most enduring symbols in modern theatre. Its power lies in its finality and its public audibility. A potential limitation, however, is that the symbol is so powerful as an endpoint that it can overshadow the complexity of what comes next. The slam criticizes the system but leaves the path forward intentionally blank."

**(8:45–9:15) TEXT 2 – IBSEN, WIDE ANALYSIS 2**  
"Considering the play’s overall architecture, Ibsen structures Nora’s journey to make this exit the inevitable, resonant climax. The entire dramatic tension builds toward this moment of sonic and visual rupture. By choosing to conclude with a sound that reverberates beyond the stage and into the audience’s space, Ibsen makes a deliberate argument about the nature of social rebellion. He suggests that challenging deeply ingrained systems like patriarchy requires a confrontation that is public and witnessed—it must be heard and felt by society to provoke reflection and, potentially, change. This contrasts sharply with Duffy’s model of private, silent escape."

**(9:15–10:00) SYNTHESIS & CONCLUSION**  
"In conclusion, both Duffy and Ibsen offer profound, yet distinct, explorations of female rebellion. Duffy illuminates agency as a **covert performance of deception**, where the oppressed use the master’s tools to dismantle his fantasy and secure their freedom. Ibsen, conversely, presents agency as an **open ideological confrontation**, a direct rejection of the script and a demand for a new language of self. Duffy’s rebellion is interior, narrative, and quiet. Ibsen’s is public, dramatic, and resonant. What unites them is the understanding that under systemic control, the assertion of selfhood is often a performative act—a deliberate choice to step out of a assigned role. Their works remind us that resistance is not monolithic; it can be the silent rewrite of a poem or the world-changing slam of a door, each a valid and powerful claim to autonomy."
